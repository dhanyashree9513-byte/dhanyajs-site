Endless problems exist actually or are we making it up ?

October 16, 2026

the experiment that started it all

In 2018, researchers David Levari and colleagues published a paper in Science exploring how people change their definitions of things when those things become less common.

One experiment asked participants to identify blue dots among a series of coloured dots.

Initially, the task was straightforward. Participants saw a range of colours and identified the ones they considered blue.

But then the researchers gradually reduced the number of blue dots.

You might expect people to identify fewer dots as blue because fewer blue dots were appearing.

Instead, something unexpected happened.

As blue dots became less common, participants started classifying dots that were previously considered purple as blue.

The definition of blue had quietly expanded.

Even when researchers explicitly told participants that blue dots were becoming less common, the effect persisted.

The participants weren't necessarily being careless. Their perception of what counted as blue was changing along with the environment.

The researchers observed related effects in other tasks involving threatening faces and unethical research proposals, suggesting that the phenomenon wasn't limited to colour perception.

The experiment revealed something surprisingly unsettling: our standards for identifying a problem can shift when the problems themselves become less common.

And we might not notice that we've changed our standards.

when fewer problems create new problems

Let's bring this into everyday life.

Imagine you're trying to improve your health.

At first, your goal is simple: exercise a few times a week and get enough sleep.

You make progress. You feel better. Your routine becomes more consistent.

But then you start following fitness influencers.

You discover people tracking their sleep stages, optimising their nutrition, monitoring their heart rate variability, and following carefully structured training plans.

Suddenly, exercising three times a week doesn't feel like enough.

Your perfectly reasonable routine starts looking inadequate beside a new standard you've been exposed to.

You haven't necessarily become less healthy.

You've acquired a different definition of what being healthy means.

Something similar can happen with money.

When you're struggling to cover your expenses, financial security might mean having enough savings to handle an emergency.

Once you achieve that, you might start comparing your savings with those of people earning more than you.

A goal that once represented security begins to feel like the bare minimum.

Then you set another target.

And another.

There's nothing inherently wrong with wanting to improve your health, earn more money, or achieve ambitious goals.

The interesting question is whether your definition of enough keeps changing every time you get closer to it.

social media gives us an endless supply of new standards

Here's where I think this phenomenon becomes especially interesting.

Our brains don't evaluate our lives in isolation. The standards we use to judge things can be influenced by what we encounter around us.

And social media gives us an almost unlimited stream of things to compare ourselves with.

You might be perfectly happy with your apartment until you see someone's beautifully designed home.

You might feel confident about your career until you see someone younger becoming a founder.

You might enjoy your weekends until everyone else's seem filled with travel, parties, and exciting experiences.

Nothing about your life necessarily changed.

But the reference points you're using to evaluate it did.

Yesterday, your apartment was comfortable.

Today, it feels small.

Yesterday, your career was progressing.

Today, it feels slow.

Yesterday, your life felt full.

Today, it feels ordinary.

It's tempting to interpret these feelings as evidence that something is missing.

Sometimes, something really is missing.

But sometimes, you've simply encountered a new standard against which your existing life looks less impressive.

And when new standards arrive faster than we can satisfy the old ones, contentment becomes difficult to maintain.

There's always another example of what could be better.

the goalposts keep moving

Think about the last time you achieved something you'd wanted for a long time.

Maybe it was getting into a particular college, landing a job, buying something expensive, or reaching a personal milestone.

Before it happened, you probably imagined how good it would feel.

And when it finally happened, perhaps it did feel wonderful.

But then, gradually, it became normal.

The achievement that once seemed extraordinary became part of your everyday life.

Your attention moved to the next thing.

Psychologists have studied related patterns through concepts such as hedonic adaptation, where people's emotional responses to positive and negative changes often diminish over time.

Prevalence-induced concept change describes something different: our criteria for identifying a category or problem can shift as its prevalence changes.

These ideas aren't interchangeable, but they can help us understand different parts of a familiar experience.

We adapt to what we achieve, and we can also change the standards we use to evaluate ourselves.

Together, these patterns raise an interesting question.

If every improvement eventually becomes the new baseline, how do we know when we've achieved enough?

Maybe the answer isn't to stop growing.

Maybe it's to become more deliberate about deciding what growth actually means to us.

does this mean we should stop trying to improve?

Not at all.

If something is genuinely making your life worse, you shouldn't ignore it just because someone else has it worse.

If you need more money to feel financially secure, pursue that goal.

If your health needs attention, take it seriously.

If your job is making you miserable, consider what needs to change.

The point isn't to convince yourself that every problem is imaginary.

It's to recognise that our standards aren't always fixed, objective measures of reality.

Sometimes, we notice a real problem.

Sometimes, we discover a new possibility.

And sometimes, we simply expand the definition of what's wrong until something that was perfectly acceptable yesterday becomes unacceptable today.

The challenge is figuring out which is happening.

One useful question is:

Did something meaningful change in my life, or did I just encounter a new standard?

If you're suddenly unhappy with your career after seeing someone else's promotion, it might be worth asking whether your own work has become less fulfilling or whether you're comparing two very different lives.

If you're unhappy with your appearance after spending hours looking at edited photographs, consider whether you've noticed a genuine concern or begun evaluating yourself against a standard you hadn't previously cared about.

Neither answer automatically invalidates your feelings.

But understanding where those feelings come from can help you decide what to do with them.

perhaps we need to redefine what counts as enough

There's a strange paradox here.

The better we become at identifying problems, the more opportunities we have to find them.

That ability is useful. It's how we recognise mistakes, improve systems, and solve difficult problems.

But applied without limits to our personal lives, it can become exhausting.

You can always find another flaw to correct, another habit to optimise, another milestone to reach.

You can turn almost any part of your life into a project.

And if you keep expanding the criteria for what counts as a problem, you'll never run out of work.

Perhaps we need to distinguish between problems that genuinely need solving and imperfections we've simply learned to notice.

Not every inefficiency needs to be eliminated.

Not every insecurity needs to become a treatment plan.

Not every ambition needs to become a deadline.

And not every part of your life needs to be better than it is today.

Sometimes, something can be imperfect without being a problem.

Your career can be a work in progress without being a failure.

Your body can be imperfect without being unacceptable.

Your life can be ordinary without being inadequate.

These aren't excuses to avoid change. They're reminders that improvement is not the same thing as constantly finding yourself lacking.

a small experiment

Here's something you can try the next time you feel dissatisfied with an area of your life.

Write down what is bothering you.

Then ask yourself three questions.

1. Was this bothering me before I started comparing myself with something else?

If the answer is no, investigate what changed in your perception.

2. If this problem disappeared tomorrow, what would count as a problem next?

Be honest. Would solving this issue genuinely improve your life, or would your attention immediately move to another imperfection?

3. What would I consider good enough if I weren't trying to keep up with anyone else?

You don't need to settle for less than you want.

You just need to know whether what you want reflects your values or an endlessly shifting standard.

The goal isn't to stop noticing things.

It's to notice when your definition of a problem is changing.

Because awareness gives you a chance to make a choice rather than automatically following the next thing that feels inadequate.

the problem with always finding a problem

I keep thinking about those coloured dots.

The dots didn't need to change for the participants to change their answers. As the distribution shifted, their definition of what counted as blue shifted, too.

And although our everyday experiences are more complicated than a laboratory experiment, the underlying idea offers a useful lens for examining how we judge our lives.

Sometimes, dissatisfaction tells us that something needs to change.

Other times, it tells us that the standards we're using to evaluate ourselves have changed.

The difficult part is knowing which one we're experiencing.

We live in a world where there's no shortage of examples of how things could be better. Better careers, better bodies, better relationships, better homes, better routines.

There's always something else to notice.

Something else to compare.

Something else to fix.

Maybe the answer isn't to stop improving our lives.

Maybe it's to occasionally pause and ask whether the thing we're trying to fix is actually a problem.

Because if our definition of what's wrong keeps expanding, we might spend our entire lives solving problems without ever feeling like we've made progress.

And that would be a rather unfortunate problem to have.

Further reading: Levari, D. E., et al. (2018). “Prevalence-induced concept change in human judgment.” Science, 360(6396), 1465–1467.
