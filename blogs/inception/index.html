concept called inception

October 15, 2026

inception: the ideas we think are our own

What if some of the things you believe about life were never really your ideas to begin with?

Have you ever stopped to wonder why you want the things you want?

Why a certain salary feels like success. Why getting married is supposed to look a particular way. Why turning thirty feels like a deadline. Why buying a house, getting promoted, travelling the world, or having a particular kind of relationship somehow became things you're supposed to achieve.

Who decided all of this?

And more importantly, when did you decide to believe it?

I recently came across an interesting idea while exploring a conversation involving Dr. Rangan Chatterjee and Jay Shetty. It reminded me of the movie Inception, where an idea is planted in someone's mind and gradually becomes so deeply embedded that the person begins to treat it as their own.

Except, in real life, nobody needs to enter your dreams to do this.

Sometimes, all it takes is an advertisement, a conversation with a friend, a social expectation, or a sentence you've heard often enough to stop questioning it.

And before you know it, you're building your life around an idea you never consciously chose.

the two-month salary rule

Here's an interesting example.

Have you ever heard that an engagement ring should cost two months' salary?

It sounds like one of those rules that has always existed. Something people simply know, like an unwritten law of adulthood.

But the idea was popularized by diamond advertising.

In their conversation, Jay Shetty describes discovering an old De Beers advertisement that promoted the notion that a diamond engagement ring should cost two months' salary. Years later, when he was preparing to propose, the same idea influenced his spending decision.

Think about what happened there.

An advertising message became a social expectation. The social expectation became a rule people repeated to one another. Eventually, someone could follow that rule without realizing where it came from.

Nobody had to force him to spend that amount.

The idea had already done its job.

That's what makes this example so fascinating. The influence doesn't necessarily feel like influence. It feels like common sense.

And once an idea becomes common sense, we rarely stop to ask who put it there.

what else have we inherited without noticing?

Let's leave engagement rings for a moment.

Think about the idea that you should have your life figured out by thirty.

Nobody sends you an official document explaining what you need to accomplish by that age. There's no universal checklist.

Yet, somewhere along the way, thirty becomes a milestone loaded with expectations.

You should have a stable career. You should be financially independent. Perhaps you should be married, own a house, or know exactly where your life is headed.

And if you're not there yet, you might start feeling as though you're behind.

Behind whom?

You might not even want some of those things right now. But seeing other people achieve them can make you question your own choices.

Or take the idea that being busy means being successful.

Someone asks how you're doing, and you say, "Busy."

It almost sounds like a badge of honour.

A packed calendar suggests importance. Working late suggests dedication. Being constantly available suggests ambition.

But is being busy necessarily the same as doing meaningful work?

Is exhaustion proof that you're making progress?

Or have we repeated these associations so often that we no longer examine them?

The same thing happens with money, beauty, relationships, career choices, and even the way we spend our free time.

We absorb ideas from our families, friends, workplaces, advertisements, films, and social media.

Some help us build lives we genuinely want.

Others become invisible expectations that we follow simply because we assume they're what everyone does.

And sometimes, we can't tell the difference.

the tricky part is that the idea feels like yours

If someone told you exactly what to think, you'd probably question them.

If an advertisement said, "You must spend two months' salary on a ring to prove that you love someone," you'd probably laugh at the absurdity.

But what if the message arrived differently?

What if you saw friends doing it? What if people you admired treated it as normal? What if every romantic film reinforced the idea that the size of the ring represented the size of the love?

Eventually, you might arrive at the same conclusion yourself.

You might even defend it.

That's the interesting part about how social expectations work. We don't always experience them as external pressure. Sometimes, they become part of our own reasoning.

We tell ourselves we want a particular job, lifestyle, possession, or milestone.

And perhaps we genuinely do.

But it is worth asking whether we want it because it aligns with our values or because we've learned to associate it with being successful, attractive, respectable, or worthy.

The distinction matters.

Not because every socially influenced desire is bad, but because there's a difference between choosing something and assuming you have to choose it.

this doesn't mean everything is manipulation

I don't think the answer is to distrust every idea we encounter.

That would be exhausting.

We're social creatures. We learn from other people, borrow their experiences, and adopt ideas that help us understand the world.

Sometimes, an idea introduced by someone else genuinely improves our lives.

A friend might convince us to exercise regularly. A book might change how we approach relationships. A conversation might help us recognize a mistake we've been making for years.

Influence isn't inherently bad.

The problem is that we don't always examine what we're absorbing.

We question the price of a product but not the desire to own it.

We compare different career paths but rarely question why a particular job title matters so much to us.

We spend hours deciding whether we're making the right choice without asking whether the choice was ever important to us in the first place.

And perhaps that's where a little more awareness could help.

Not by rejecting every expectation, but by becoming curious about the ones we follow automatically.

a small question to ask yourself

The next time you find yourself desperately wanting something, pause for a moment.

It could be a promotion, a new phone, a particular body type, a relationship milestone, or a lifestyle you've seen someone else living.

Ask yourself:

Would I still want this if nobody else knew I had it?

Imagine there were no pictures to post, no compliments to receive, no relatives to impress, and no friends to compare yourself with.

Would the thing itself still matter to you?

Would the experience still make you happy?

Would the achievement still feel meaningful?

You might discover that the answer is yes.

You genuinely want the job because the work interests you. You want the house because you value having a place of your own. You want the ring because you love it, regardless of what it costs.

Wonderful.

But you might also discover that some of your desires become less compelling when you remove the audience.

Perhaps you don't actually want to be busy. You want to feel important.

Perhaps you don't want the expensive thing. You want the feeling of having made it.

Perhaps you're not worried about turning thirty. You're worried about what other people might think if your life looks different from theirs.

These aren't comfortable realizations, necessarily.

But they're useful ones.

Because once you recognize the difference between what you want and what you think you should want, you have a little more freedom to decide what deserves your time, money, and attention.

what if we became more careful about the ideas we let in?

We spend a lot of time thinking about the decisions we make.

Perhaps we should spend a little more time thinking about the beliefs behind those decisions.

Where did this idea come from?

Who benefits if I believe it?

Does it reflect my values, or have I simply heard it often enough to accept it?

And if I no longer believed it, would I make the same choices?

You don't need to answer these questions every time you buy something or make a life decision.

But occasionally, they might reveal something important.

Because the most powerful ideas aren't always the ones that persuade us in a single moment.

Sometimes, they're the ones that quietly shape our definition of a good life over many years.

We follow them when choosing careers, spending money, entering relationships, setting goals, and deciding whether we're doing well enough.

We may never remember where they came from.

We just remember that they're supposed to be true.

And perhaps that's why the concept of inception is so interesting outside the movies.

An idea doesn't need to be hidden inside a dream to influence your reality. It only needs to become part of the story you tell yourself about how life is supposed to work.

The difficult part is recognizing it.

The liberating part is realizing that you can question it.

Maybe the goal isn't to live a life untouched by other people's ideas. Maybe it's to become aware enough to decide which of those ideas deserve a place in yours.
