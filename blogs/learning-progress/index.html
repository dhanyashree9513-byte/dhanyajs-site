the illusion of preparing to learn

October 19, 2026

the illusion of preparing to learn

You decide to learn something new. Maybe it's AI, a language, or a skill you've been putting off for months.

You start by searching for where to begin.

One video recommends a beginner's course. Another offers a complete roadmap. Someone shares a list of the ten things you need to know before getting started. You save them all, open a few tabs, and promise yourself you'll start properly over the weekend.

Then you discover an even better resource.

Before you know it, you've spent hours figuring out how to learn something without actually learning much of it.

It's a strange kind of productivity. You feel closer to your goal because you've organized the path towards it. But if someone asked you what you'd learned, you might struggle to point to anything concrete.

the comfort of getting ready

Preparing to learn feels good because it gives us a sense of control.

The resources are neatly saved. The plan looks achievable. We have a rough idea of what comes next, and the uncertainty of beginning seems a little less intimidating.

Actually learning, however, is messier.

You misunderstand things. You forget what you read. You try something and get it wrong. You discover that a concept that seemed simple in a video is surprisingly difficult to understand on your own.

Planning lets us imagine the person we'll become. Practice forces us to confront where we are today.

And sometimes, without realizing it, we choose the activity that makes us feel capable over the one that helps us become capable.

when the roadmap becomes the destination

AI is an interesting example of this.

You hear about ChatGPT, AI assistants, and tools that can write, create images, or answer questions. You're curious, so you decide to understand what all the excitement is about.

Suddenly, you're encountering unfamiliar terms, endless courses, and people claiming you need to learn everything from coding to machine learning to build a career in AI.

It's easy to feel that you need to understand the entire field before you can begin.

But do you?

You could start by using an AI tool to plan a trip, explain a difficult concept, or help you organize a task. You could compare its answer with what you already know. You could ask the same question in different ways and notice how the responses change.

You don't need to understand how an engine works to begin learning how to drive a car.

Of course, using AI is not the same as understanding how it works. If you want to build AI systems or pursue a career in the field, deeper technical learning matters. But even then, you can learn progressively instead of waiting until you've collected every resource you might ever need.

The best starting point isn't necessarily the most comprehensive roadmap. It's often a small, concrete thing you can try today.

start before you feel completely ready

There's nothing wrong with planning. A good plan saves time and helps you stay focused.

The problem begins when planning becomes a substitute for doing.

So the next time you decide to learn something, try a simple rule: spend less time searching for the perfect resource and more time working with the ones you already have.

Watch the lesson, then try to explain what you learned without looking at it. Read about a concept, then find a way to use it. Explore a new tool, then attempt a task on your own.

Let your questions emerge from experience rather than trying to anticipate every question before you start.

You might discover that you need a different resource after all. That's fine. The difference is that now you know why you need it.

You don't have to finish preparing for the journey before you take the first step. Sometimes, the clearest roadmap appears only after you've started walking.
