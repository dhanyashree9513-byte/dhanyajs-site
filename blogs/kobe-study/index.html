the person you become when nobody's watching

the person you become when nobody's watching

on Kobe Bryant, alter egos, and learning to get out of your own way.

There's a version of you that knows what to do.

The version that speaks up in the meeting instead of rehearsing the sentence in their head for twenty minutes. The version that starts the thing instead of waiting to feel ready. The version that doesn't crumble the moment something gets uncomfortable.

You've probably met this person before.

Maybe on a really good day. Maybe when you were so absorbed in something that you forgot to worry about how you were being perceived.

And then, for reasons you can't quite explain, you go back to being yourself.

Or at least, the version of yourself you're used to.

What if you could access that other version more deliberately?

That's where this story gets interesting.

enter the Black Mamba

If you've heard of Kobe Bryant, you've probably heard of the Black Mamba.

But the Black Mamba wasn't just a nickname. It was a separate identity Kobe used to approach basketball with a particular mindset: focused, relentless, composed, and ready to compete.

Kobe developed the Black Mamba persona during a difficult period in his life and career. The name was inspired by the snake in Kill Bill, and over time, it became associated with the way he approached the game.

Performance coach Todd Herman later discussed the role of the Black Mamba in his work on the Alter Ego Effect.

The idea is fascinating because Kobe wasn't trying to become a completely different person.

He was creating a way to access particular qualities when he needed them most.

Think about that for a second.

The same person could experience doubt, frustration, and the ordinary messiness of being human, yet step onto the court with a very different sense of purpose.

What changed?

Not necessarily his abilities in that moment. Not his entire personality.

The way he chose to show up.

we all have a story about ourselves

Think about the things you casually say about yourself.

"I'm not a confident person."

"I'm terrible at speaking in public."

"I always procrastinate."

"I'm just not someone who takes risks."

At some point, these statements might have started as observations. Maybe you had a bad experience, failed at something, or found a particular situation uncomfortable.

But repeat a story about yourself enough times, and it starts to feel like a permanent fact.

You stop saying, "I'm nervous about public speaking."

You start saying, "I'm not a public speaker."

The first describes a feeling. The second turns that feeling into an identity.

And once you believe something is part of who you are, you might unconsciously behave in ways that keep proving it true.

You avoid the presentation. You let someone else lead. You wait until you're more confident.

Which means you get fewer opportunities to discover that you could have handled it.

It's a surprisingly effective way to keep yourself exactly where you are.

what if you borrowed a different identity?

This is the central idea behind Todd Herman's Alter Ego Effect.

Instead of asking, "How do I stop being this person?" you ask:

"Who do I need to be in this particular moment?"

Notice the difference.

You're not trying to erase your personality or manufacture an entirely new human being.

You're identifying the qualities a situation requires and finding a way to bring them forward.

Imagine you're someone who has great ideas but struggles to share them at work.

You could spend months telling yourself to be more confident.

Or you could ask what a confident version of you would actually do.

Maybe they'd speak before the perfect sentence appeared in their head. Maybe they'd ask the question instead of worrying whether it sounded stupid. Maybe they'd share an unfinished idea and let the conversation improve it.

Give this version of yourself a name if that helps. Create a small ritual that signals it's time to step into that role.

Then practise behaving accordingly.

The name isn't magic. The useful part is the deliberate shift from thinking about your limitations to choosing your next action.

You already know what confidence looks like in other people. You might even recognize moments when you've demonstrated it yourself.

The question is whether you can learn to access those behaviours when your usual instincts tell you to hold back.

the interesting bit: you're not faking it

At first, the idea of having an alter ego can sound a little ridiculous.

Do I really need to give myself a superhero name to get things done?

Absolutely not.

But there's a useful distinction here.

Faking confidence means trying to convince yourself that you're never afraid.

Using an alter ego means accepting that you're afraid and choosing how you want to act anyway.

You can be nervous and still speak.

You can doubt your abilities and still submit the application.

You can feel completely unprepared and still sit down to learn.

The feeling doesn't have to disappear before the action becomes possible.

And that's important because we often treat our emotions as instructions.

If I feel nervous, I shouldn't do it.

If I feel unqualified, I'm probably not ready.

If I feel uncomfortable, perhaps this isn't for me.

But feelings are information, not always accurate predictions of what will happen next.

Sometimes you don't need to feel like a different person.

You just need to stop letting the person you think you are make every decision.

try it in your own life

You don't need to be an athlete or have a stadium full of people watching you to experiment with this.

Start with one situation where you tend to hold yourself back.

Maybe it's interviews. Maybe it's creating something publicly. Maybe it's having a difficult conversation or finally starting a project you've been thinking about for months.

Ask yourself three questions.

1. What usually gets in my way?

Be specific. Not "I'm lazy," but "I keep checking my phone when a task becomes difficult."

Not "I'm bad at interviews," but "I rush my answers because I'm afraid of silence."

2. What qualities would help me handle this situation?

Perhaps you need patience, curiosity, decisiveness, courage, or the willingness to look inexperienced while learning something new.

Choose behaviours, not vague personality traits.

3. What would I do differently if I embodied those qualities for the next 30 minutes?

Maybe you'd put your phone away and finish one task.

Maybe you'd answer the interview question slowly, even if you needed a moment to think.

Maybe you'd publish the article before you found another reason to rewrite the opening.

Then do that.

You can create a name, choose a symbolic object, or build a small ritual around the transition if you find it useful. Those are optional tools, not requirements.

The point is to turn an abstract idea about who you want to become into something you can actually practise.

And remember: an alter ego isn't a replacement for preparation, skill, or experience. It can help you approach the work differently, but you still have to do the work.

you don't have to be one fixed version of yourself

I think this is the part that extends far beyond basketball.

We spend so much time trying to discover who we are, as if there's one final answer waiting somewhere inside us.

I'm introverted. I'm not ambitious. I'm bad with money. I'm not creative. I'm not a leader.

Some of these descriptions might capture real preferences or patterns. But they don't have to become permanent boundaries around what we're willing to try.

You can be quiet and learn to lead.

You can be afraid of failure and still build something.

You can be a beginner at thirty, forty, or sixty without treating it as evidence that you've missed your chance.

You can behave differently in different situations without being fake.

You're allowed to be a work in progress.

And perhaps the most useful thing about the alter ego idea is that it gives you permission to experiment with yourself.

Instead of asking whether you're naturally confident, ask whether you can practise acting with confidence.

Instead of deciding that you're not disciplined, ask what it would look like to follow through on one small commitment today.

You don't have to settle the question of who you are before you start changing how you behave.

Sometimes, the evidence comes afterward.

You do the difficult thing. You survive it. You do it again.

And slowly, the story you tell yourself begins to change.

Not because you gave yourself a cool name, but because you have new experiences to draw from.

maybe you already have that version in you

I keep coming back to the idea that we might be more flexible than the stories we tell ourselves suggest.

The confident version. The curious version. The version that takes risks, asks questions, speaks honestly, or keeps going when something gets hard.

Perhaps these aren't entirely different people waiting to be invented.

Perhaps they're possibilities we haven't learned to access consistently.

Kobe Bryant's Black Mamba offers an interesting example of deliberately separating the demands of a particular role from the rest of your identity. Todd Herman's work turns that idea into a framework people can experiment with in their own lives.

It isn't a guarantee of extraordinary performance. And it doesn't mean every limitation can be overcome by changing your mindset.

But it offers a useful question to carry around.

The next time you catch yourself saying, "I'm just not that kind of person," pause for a second.

Is that actually a limit?

Or is it a story you've repeated so often that you've stopped testing it?

What if you don't need to become someone else? What if you just need to give a different part of yourself a chance to show up?
