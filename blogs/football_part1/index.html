Understanding Football: The Rules, the Players, and the Beautiful Chaos

October 22, 2026

An introduction to the world's most popular sport, for anyone who has ever watched a match and wondered what was going on.

There is something fascinating about football.

Twenty-two players run around a field, chasing a ball, while thousands of people in a stadium—and millions more watching from home—hold their breath over every pass, tackle, and shot. A player scores a goal, and suddenly an entire stadium erupts. A referee blows a whistle, and half the crowd celebrates while the other half protests. Sometimes, a team dominates an entire match but loses because of one moment.

If you are new to football, all of this can seem a little confusing.

Why does the referee sometimes stop the game? What exactly is offside? Why do players receive yellow and red cards? Why does a match end in a draw even when one team seems much better than the other?

And perhaps the biggest question: why do people become so emotionally attached to a game that can make them incredibly happy one day and completely miserable the next?

Let's start at the beginning.

1. What exactly is football?

Football, known as soccer in some countries, is a team sport in which two teams compete to score goals by getting a ball into the opposing team's goal.

Each team normally has eleven players on the field, including a goalkeeper. The goalkeeper is the only player who can generally handle the ball with their hands, but only within their own penalty area and subject to the rules.

Everyone else must use their feet, head, chest, or other permitted parts of the body to control and move the ball. Using the hands or arms deliberately is generally a foul.

The objective is wonderfully simple: score more goals than the other team before time runs out.

A standard match lasts 90 minutes, divided into two halves of 45 minutes each. There is usually a short interval between the halves.

However, the clock does not always stop when the ball goes out of play or a player gets injured. Instead, the referee adds time at the end of each half to account for delays. This is called added time or stoppage time.

So when you see a match reach 90 minutes and the scoreboard display 90+5, it means the referee has allowed approximately five additional minutes.

The exact amount of time played can vary because the referee decides how much time needs to be added.

And that is your first important lesson about football: the clock may say 90 minutes, but the drama does not necessarily end there.

2. Meet the players: who does what on the field?

At first glance, football players may look as though they are all doing the same thing—running after the ball. In reality, different players have different responsibilities.

Understanding their positions makes a match much easier to follow.

The goalkeeper

The goalkeeper protects the goal.

Their job is to stop the opposing team from scoring by saving shots, catching crosses, and organising the defenders in front of them.

A good goalkeeper can change the outcome of a match. One extraordinary save can be just as important as a goal.

Defenders

Defenders protect their team's goal and try to prevent the opposition from creating scoring opportunities.

Some defenders play centrally, close to the goalkeeper. Others play on the left or right sides of the field.

Their responsibilities include tackling opponents, blocking shots, intercepting passes, and helping the team move the ball forward.

Modern defenders are often expected to do much more than defend. They may start attacks with accurate passes or move forward to support their teammates.

Midfielders

Midfielders operate between the defence and the attack.

Think of them as the connecting bridge between the two.

They help win possession, pass the ball, control the pace of the game, and create opportunities for attackers.

Some midfielders concentrate on defending and breaking up opposition attacks. Others focus on creating chances with clever passes. Some are expected to do both.

If you watch a player who constantly seems to be involved in everything, from winning the ball to setting up a goal, they might be a midfielder.

Forwards and attackers

Attackers are primarily responsible for creating and scoring goals.

Some play centrally and are known as strikers. Others operate on the wings, using speed, dribbling, and crosses to create opportunities.

A striker might spend much of a match waiting for the perfect pass before suddenly scoring. A winger might beat two defenders and deliver a cross that leads to a goal.

Of course, modern football is flexible. Attackers defend, defenders attack, and midfielders can become goal scorers.

The positions describe a player's main responsibilities, not strict boundaries.

3. How does a football match actually work?

A football match begins with a kickoff.

After the referee signals for play to begin, the teams try to control possession, move the ball into dangerous areas, and create chances to score.

The ball remains in play until it completely crosses a boundary line or the referee stops play for another reason.

If the ball crosses either of the long boundary lines, called the touchlines, the opposing team gets a throw-in.

If it crosses the goal line at the end of the field without resulting in a goal, the restart depends on which team touched it last and where it crossed.

There are three common possibilities:

  • →Throw-in: The ball leaves the field over a touchline.
  • →Goal kick: The attacking team last touched the ball before it crossed the defending team's goal line without a goal being scored.
  • →Corner kick: The defending team last touched the ball before it crossed its own goal line without a goal being scored.

A corner kick is particularly exciting because the attacking team gets an opportunity to send the ball into the penalty area, where teammates can attempt to score with their heads or feet.

A goal kick, meanwhile, allows the defending team to restart play from its goal area.

These restarts may seem like small details, but they are important parts of the game. A team can create a dangerous scoring opportunity from something as ordinary as a throw-in or corner.

4. The offside rule: football's most confusing concept

If there is one rule that confuses new football fans more than any other, it is offside.

The good news is that the basic idea is not as complicated as it first appears.

Imagine that an attacking player is standing very close to the opposing goalkeeper, waiting for a teammate to pass them the ball. The player is effectively waiting behind the defenders rather than trying to get past them.

Without the offside rule, attackers could simply stand beside the goal for the entire match and wait for long passes.

Offside helps prevent this and encourages more organised attacking and defending.

Here is the basic principle.

A player can be penalised for being in an offside position when a teammate plays or touches the ball if, at that moment, the player is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opposing player—and the player is then involved in active play.

There are important details to remember:

  • →Being in an offside position is not automatically an offence.
  • →The player must become involved in active play for an offside offence to occur.
  • →The player's position is assessed when a teammate plays or touches the ball, not when the pass reaches them.
  • →A player cannot be offside in their own half.
  • →There is no offside offence directly from a goal kick, throw-in, or corner kick.

The second-last opponent is often a defender, but it does not have to be. The goalkeeper is frequently the last opponent, which is why commentators often talk about the last defender.

Let's make this practical.

Suppose a striker is standing behind the opposing defence. A teammate passes the ball forward, and the striker runs onto it. If the striker was in an offside position when the teammate played the ball and then becomes involved in play, the referee may award an indirect free kick to the defending team.

Now imagine the striker times the run perfectly and starts moving forward from an onside position when the pass is played. Even if they end up alone in front of the goalkeeper, the goal can be perfectly legal.

That is why you will sometimes see a striker celebrate a goal only for the assistant referee to raise a flag.

Video Assistant Referee technology, commonly called VAR, can help officials review certain decisions in competitions that use it. However, not every competition uses the same technology or review arrangements.

The easiest way to understand offside is to remember this: it is about the attacker's position when the ball is played, not simply about being ahead of the defenders when receiving it.

5. Fouls, free kicks, and penalty kicks

Football is a physical sport, but not every physical challenge is a foul.

Players are allowed to compete for the ball. They are not allowed to trip, push, hold, or unfairly charge opponents in ways prohibited by the Laws of the Game.

The referee decides whether an incident is a foul and what punishment is appropriate.

Free kicks

When a foul occurs, the opposing team may receive a free kick.

There are two main types.

A direct free kick allows a player to score directly by kicking the ball into the goal.

An indirect free kick requires the ball to touch another player before a goal can be scored.

For many free kicks, defenders must stand at least 9.15 metres, or 10 yards, from the ball until it is in play, subject to the specific rules for the situation.

You may have seen players form a defensive wall in front of a free kick. The wall is meant to block the direct route to the goal.

Penalty kicks

A penalty is awarded when a player commits a direct-free-kick offence inside their own penalty area while the ball is in play, provided the offence is punishable by a penalty.

The penalty area is the large marked rectangle surrounding each goal.

The ball is placed on the penalty mark, 11 metres from the goal line, and one attacking player takes the shot against the goalkeeper.

A penalty is one of football's most dramatic moments. The taker has a clear opportunity to score, but the goalkeeper can still make a save.

And sometimes, the entire match seems to come down to a single kick.

Advantage

Sometimes, a referee sees a foul but allows play to continue because stopping the game would benefit the team that committed it.

This is called playing the advantage.

For example, if a player is fouled but their teammate immediately gains possession and has an excellent chance to attack, the referee may let the move continue.

The referee can still take disciplinary action at the next stoppage when appropriate.

This is one reason why you should not assume that every foul will be followed by an immediate whistle.

6. Yellow cards and red cards

Cards are the referee's way of dealing with misconduct.

A yellow card is a caution. It is a formal warning for an offence serious enough to deserve disciplinary action.

Players may receive yellow cards for things such as reckless challenges, unsporting behaviour, persistent fouling, or dissent.

A red card means the player is sent off and must leave the field. Their team generally cannot replace them, so it continues with one fewer player.

A player can receive a red card directly for a serious offence or for receiving a second yellow card in the same match.

A red card can completely change the course of a game. A team that was controlling possession may suddenly have to defend with ten players instead of eleven.

That does not mean it will automatically lose, but the challenge becomes much harder.

Cards can also matter beyond a single match. Depending on the competition's rules, accumulated yellow cards or a red card may lead to a suspension for a future game.

7. How do teams win a league match?

In most league competitions, a win earns three points, a draw earns one point for each team, and a defeat earns no points.

Let's imagine a team has played five matches:

| Result | Points earned | |---|---:| | Win | 3 | | Win | 3 | | Draw | 1 | | Loss | 0 | | Win | 3 | | Total | 10 |

The team has earned ten points.

Over a season, these points determine where each team stands in the league table.

If two teams finish level on points, the competition's rules determine how they are separated. In many major leagues, goal difference is an important factor.

Goal difference is calculated by subtracting the number of goals a team has conceded from the number it has scored.

For example, a team that scores 60 goals and concedes 30 has a goal difference of +30.

A team that scores 45 and concedes 25 has a goal difference of +20.

If both teams have the same number of points, the team with the better goal difference may finish higher, depending on the competition's rules.

This is why a team might continue attacking even when it is already winning. An additional goal could matter at the end of the season.

8. What happens if a match is tied?

The answer depends on the competition.

In a league match, a draw is usually an acceptable result. Both teams receive a point, and the match ends without a winner.

In a knockout competition, however, one team often needs to advance.

Depending on the tournament's rules, a tied match may go to extra time, a penalty shootout, or directly to penalties.

Extra time consists of two periods of 15 minutes each.

If the score remains level after extra time, a penalty shootout may decide the winner. Players from each team take penalties in turn, and the team that scores more in the initial series wins. If the teams remain level, the shootout continues in sudden-death rounds until one team has scored more after the same number of kicks.

A penalty shootout is different from the penalties awarded during normal play. It is a separate method of deciding the winner when a knockout match cannot remain tied.

Some competitions use different procedures, so the tournament's rules always matter.

9. Why does football look so different from one match to another?

You might watch one match where both teams attack relentlessly and another where almost nothing seems to happen.

That is because football is not just about individual skill. It is also about tactics.

A team may choose to press aggressively, trying to win the ball back immediately after losing it.

Another may defend deep, keep its players close together, and wait for an opportunity to counterattack.

Some teams prefer to maintain possession and patiently create openings through short passes. Others use long passes, speed, and physical strength to attack quickly.

These are different ways of trying to solve the same problem: how to score goals while preventing the opposition from scoring.

A team's tactics may also change during a match.

If it is winning, it may focus on protecting its lead. If it is losing, it may send more players forward. If an important player is injured or sent off, the manager may need to change the entire approach.

Football is therefore a game of constant decisions.

Every pass, run, substitution, and tactical adjustment can affect what happens next.

10. The most important thing to remember

You do not need to understand every rule before you can enjoy football.

Start by following the ball, noticing which team has possession, and watching how the players move when they do not have it.

Look at how a team creates space, how defenders respond to an attack, and how players react after losing the ball.

You will gradually recognise the patterns.

Soon, you may find yourself noticing that a midfielder is making a clever run, that a defender is keeping an attacker onside, or that a team is pressing much higher than it did earlier in the game.

The rules will become familiar through watching.

And somewhere along the way, you may stop watching simply to understand what is happening. You may start watching because you desperately want a particular team to win.

That is when football begins to feel different.

Because understanding the game is only the beginning. The next step is discovering the enormous world of leagues, cups, rivalries, and international tournaments that gives football its year-round drama.s
